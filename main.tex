\section{Abstract}

Live coding -as defined by \textit{Alex McLean}, one of the most relevant figures in the field- is the practice of constructing and
interacting with algorithms during a performance. It aims to convey algorithmic thinking, real-time composition and networked collaborations to the
audience.\cite{usermanual}
 A distinctive quality of improvisation is the temporal element, 
 which involves interacting with tools and the capacity to rethink rules and conditions.
  There is no concern on optimization and efficiency -features that typically characterize the technology industry-, 
  and instead there is a focus on embracing error, experimentation, and open processes rather than generating outcomes.

In this thesis, I will review the different experiences that I have had in relation to my own live coding practice. I want to share the methodologies I have learned and discovered along my journey as a live coder. I will delve into my research on time in live coding, its playful nature, and how the community plays a fundamental role in consolidating an open source and diverse practice that aims to explore the computer as an expressive interface.

In the first part, I will go into more depth on certain technical and theoretical aspects of live coding, as well as a brief overview of the history of live coding and the most recent contributions made by the community in terms of developing new tools, experiments, and connections between different disciplines such as cinema.

Then, in the second part, I will talk about different performances and conceptual and technical concepts, as well as my experience co-organizing Toplap Linz, a community dedicated to the exchange of knowledge and experimentation in live coding.

Finally, I would like to share the documentation of the performances, photographs, and videos,
 as well as an interactive website that includes the code for some of the performances, 
 where you can experience an excerpt of what happened and also access the code, allowing visitors 
 to the website to copy, use, modify, and perhaps understand it.

\subparagraph{Key words} Audio-Visual livecoding, collaborative live coding, communities and artistic practices,
algorithmic thinking, and real-time composition.

\section{Introduction}


My interest in programming was a coincidence. I had finished my bachelor's 
degree in fine arts and had mostly worked with "analog" techniques (which means that computer art was something foreign to me).
 Af  ter my time at the art academy, I found myself with the terrifying freedom of no longer being in an institution. 
 It was at that moment that I began working with \textit{Juana Prieto}, and the two of us started 
 this journey into the field of creative coding.

We had done our undergraduate studies together and had formed a friendship since then.
 \textit{Juana} had the idea to make a GIF movie for the web — the text would lead to new places, 
 a linear narrative that ultimately became a labyrinth of moving images and blinking text. 
 Neither of us had extensive knowledge of programming, but I joined the project because the idea presented itself as a challenge that combined things that intrigued me, like the relationship between image and text, where both are equally important.

We spent months working with the image and text archives we each had, merging narratives and creating 
labyrinths together. Those were months of discovery for both of us. We had received support
 from the \textit{Cinemateca of Bogotá} and were working in the institution’s labs. For that project, we used \textit{Twine}, which is a mix of HTML and its own CSS-like style. As the weeks went by and we read the documentation, we began using \textit{JavaScript} within the program to add some logic and elements that made sense within the web aesthetic — like decision boxes or having the audience answer questions.

From that moment on, programming stopped being an unattainable mystery reserved for engineers and computer scientists. I began to explore the potential of this language used to communicate with machines. While we were at the\textit{ Cinemateca in Bogotá}, we came across \textit{Toplap Bogotá}, a live coding community. \textit{RafroBeat} (who is also a member of \textit{Toplap}) was giving a workshop on \textit{Tidal Cycles} at the time, which marked the beginning of my journey with code as a performative act.

The week after that workshop, \textit{Malitzin Cortés and Iván Abreu}, two Mexican artists who work with code, architecture,
 and expanded cinema, gave a workshop where we used film archives as a 
 base to create live coding performances. 
 That was my first experience performing in front of an audience, and the first time \textit{Juana} and I performed together.

Together, we created the collective \textit{Vestidas y Alborotadas (Clothed and Rowdy)} (Figure 1)
 and became active members of the \textit{Toplap} community. We performed numerous times in \textit{Bogotá}, \textit{Medellín}, and \textit{Manizales}. 
 Together, we questioned the presence of the body on stage. And from that question,
In each performance, we embodied a different monster through the design of our costumes, 
which were made from upcycled textiles. 

Our intention was always to make people dance with our fast-paced music, using samples from our own voices and the sounds of our surroundings. 
We used our archive of photos and videos to create visuals. With each performance, we offered the audience a glimpse into the world of a different monster, complete with its own sounds and images, creating a narrative around these fictional beings. 
We didn't want to be perceived as "the girls" on stage in a predominantly masculine space, so we chose to be scary, weird, and monstrous in each performance.

In each performance of \textit{Vestidas y Alborotadas}, as well as wearing monster costumes, we created a series of images and videos of these monsters posing in different ways or in different locations around the city, either walking or standing still. We also liked to incorporate city sounds into our performances. Through these, we aimed to extend the actions of the monsters we embodied on stage. These monsters were a composition completed by visual and sonic elements, as well as our presence as these beings.

When I arrived at the \textit{Kunstuniversität Linz}, 
I found an exciting musical and artistic scene in the city. 
The art scene, both inside and outside the university, 
thrilled me with its openness the spaces and the community are 
genuinely willing to see and listen to what you do. Over time, 
together with \textit{Gorka Egino} (also a student at \textit{Kunstuniversität Linz} and a livecoder), and with the support of the \textit{Noise Meetup Lab}, 
we decided to create a live coding platform in \textit{Linz} called \textit{Toplap Linz}. 
Through a series of workshops and jam sessions, 
we aimed to build a space for sharing knowledge and fostering community.

The creative or inventive capacity of coding resides in its more human side, in contrast to the process-optimization mindset we usually associate with code.\cite{GenerativeaRT} Here writing code is not about debugging but about expression and performativity of algorithmic thought.
 While it is a language that requires precision to be executed, its potential to expand the written word is unique.

Live coding is a practice in which the programmer writes their own writing machine. These are open machines, supported by a community.
 There are always collaborations, rewritings, updates, and improvements. And as live coding, artist-programmer 
 \textit{Alex McLean} states: ``A program is not programmed to be used later,
 but is used by programming it.''\cite{meaningLife}.

  But going back to the basics, what is live coding? As I will present it later 
  in more detail, one of the most common definitions is that "it is a practice of programming in front of an audience, where the code is executed in real time, 
  and the performer interacts with the code as part of the performance".\cite{cats}
  This definition, however, does not encompass all the nuances and possibilities of this practice.
  
  We would have to go deeper into the unfolding of the code within the performative act,
  the characterization of some Domain Specific Languages (DSLs), the playfulness within the scene,
  and the community that has been built around this practice, 
  and its open source philosophy. Liveness has more to do with making a political statement than with meeting a technical requirement.

 This thesis explores the concept of \textit{liveness} in live coding, whether in solo or collaborative performances, on-site or online, and how the code is written and executed in real time. In this work, I will discuss my experience of live coding, my involvement in \textit{Toplap Linz} as a co-founder, and the various methodologies and performances I have encountered on this journey. Some of the questions I want to address are: How does time exist in this on-the-fly programming?
 How do invention and intervention lead to playfulness? Why is it important to keep the code alive, and how does this approach become a political statement?

\subsection{Software art }


In order to understand live coding, it is first necessary to discuss software art and its evolution, as these two disciplines are closely related.  This artistic approach considers software beyond its practical tool definition, shifting the focus to the expressiveness of the medium itself. Works of art explore the materiality of software and computer networks. As media theorist Tilman Baumgärtel states:

\begin{adjustwidth}{1.27cm}{1.27cm}Software art is not art that has been created with the help of a
computer but art that happens in the computer. Software is not
programmed by artists, in order to produce autonomous work,
but the software itself is the artwork. What is crucial here is not
the result but the process triggered in the computer by the
program  code. \cite [p.1--2]{baumgartel2001experimentelle}.
\end{adjustwidth}


The word \textit{software} in the field of arts first appeared in an exhibition called \textit{Software: Information Technology: Its New Meaning for Art}, curated by artist and critic Jack Burnham, at the Jewish Museum in New York between 1970-1971. According to Burnham, the exhibition took software out of the field of engineering and emphasized its relationship to systems, data, and processes in the representation of artistic expressions and ideas.
\cite{katz1970software}

Another example of how software art began to gain more presence in festivals and exhibitions was when \textit{Transmediale}\footnote{Transmediale is a Berlin-based festival and year-round project that draws out new connections between art, culture, and technology. It considers technology as being more than the digital world and the cultural as being more than what emerges from within institutionalized fields of production.} introduced "artistic software" as a new category in 2001.  Also, in 2003, \textit{Ars Electronica}, one of the biggest digital art festivals, focused its attention on code under the theme of \textit{Code—The Language of Our Time.} This theme was approached as both artistic and political expression, reflecting on topics such as privacy and aesthetics related to software. 
\cite{broeckmann2006software}

%Aclarar en que se relaciona software art con el código
Artist-coder and researcher \textit{Soon Winnie} described the characteristics of 
software art as "rules, languages, programmability, and the live conditions of execution processes" \cite{soon2018executing}. 
These characteristics are also the main materialities that have been subjects of artistic experimentation, analysis from a sociological perspective, as in digital humanities, or technological development in the field of engineering. In the artistic field, software is not only a tool for producing autonomous works of art or expressions, but it is also "using code as an expression itself" (Andersen  Pold, 2004b).
\cite{andersen2004software}

%encuentra el lugar para definir algoritmo bien sea aqui o despues de la parte que habla de codigo

According to the \textit{IEEE Standard Glossary of Software Engineering Terminology}, code is defined as: “Computer instructions and data definitions expressed in a programming language or in a form output by an assembler, compiler, or other translator.” \cite{ieee1983ieee} Computers are multilingual, and these languages enable communication between humans and machines. This has involved the development of human-readable, problem-oriented coding languages. Professor of Art and Computation Geoff Cox states the following about the two-dimensional nature of the code: “Code lies in its execution, not simply its written form. To fully appreciate it, we need to 'see' the code in order to grasp what we are experiencing and build an understanding of its actions.” \cite{cox2001aesthetics} As well as existing in written form, code exists as possible actions within a computer system. This makes it possible to see the different outputs generated by these written instructions and interactions with various machines, systems, networks, and databases.

\textit{The Book After the Book} is a net art piece that reflects on the expressiveness of code and
 the transformations of literature in the context of the internet. It was published by brazilian media artist \textit{Giselle Beiguelman} in 1999.  This work of art is a "new reading machine" in which knowledge is not 
 organized as it was in the past, when librarians classified and created categories to arrange books within a 
 physical space. Rather, this space functions "as a node in a network." In this webpage, one can wander through
  hyperlinks to different bookshelves that compile lists of literary or philosophical texts, net art, and, 
  in turn, it is a self-referential work that considers its own existence and its role in digital spaces. 
\cite{oliver2019digital}
  
In the section "Frictions" of The \textit{Book After the Book}, you can read this statement about the text behind the webpage, questioning its aesthetic nature and the specifics of this language hidden in the backend of this digital medium:


\begin{adjustwidth}{1.27cm}{1.27cm} Deep down, there on the back of the page, at the source, a situation is defined: the Internet
 is no more than a big text. But what happens when this text emerges, when it occupies the surface of the screen? 
 A strange passage is imposed: from screen to screen, the letter migrates, 
 it de-contextualises itself, posing language as an aesthetic problem. Given that, one asks: is this literature or not?  
\cite{beiguelman2019museums}
\end{adjustwidth}

 \textit{Beiguelman} addresses the different computational layers in which code is present, being invisible as written instructions but giving life to the functioning of the web. It also asks about the materiality of the code itself, its aesthetic dimensions, and the reading act in digital and hybrid spaces beyond the printed medium.

 Some artists in the field of software art have become interested in the concept of liveness, 
 making use of "real-time computation, live data, and dynamic systems" \cite{soon2018executing}.  One work that uses real-time computation and takes the Internet Network as its main source is \textit{Listening Post} (2000-2001) 
 by statistician \textit{Mark Hansen} and sound artist \textit{Ben Rubin} (Figure 2). In this work, more than 200 small screens show fragments of text from thousands of Internet chat rooms, bulletin boards, and public forums. The text sequence begins with the phrases "I am," "I like," or "I love."  A voice synthesizer reads some of the texts appearing on the screens simultaneously.

  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{listening.jpeg}
  \begin{subfigure}{0.8\textwidth}

 In this work, we encounter a system that operates autonomously. The running machine does not require human intervention 
 and creates a random composition of the data it incessantly collects. However, this autonomy is ultimately driven by human concepts and ideas. Performance studies scholar \textit{Philip Auslander} asks, "If Listening Post is a performance, who or what is the performer? 
 (…) Do machines perform?". \cite{auslander2005listening} This question is important for considering liveness beyond the human experience. It makes us consider technological processes that are hidden behind the work and its visual and sonic representation, such as data transmission, networking, and processing. 
 
In the context of media art and technology, \textit{Auslander} defined "live" as a representation in which the feedback 
given by the system or machine is almost immediate.
 Live is actually an effect of mediatization [...] it was the development of recording technologies that 
 made it possible to perceive existing representation as live \cite{auslander2022liveness}.  
 For example, in the case of \textit{Listening Post}, we have a multimedia representation of a source of dynamic data 
 (in this case, the flow of text collected from different sites on the internet). 
 Scholar \textit{Rita Raley} discusses \textit{Listening Post}, noting that this installation captures the sentiment of the virtual crowd with information and voices in constant change, with approximately 100,000 real-time messages. She points out that, even if there is actually a delay of one or two hours (meaning it is not entirely real-time), the sensation of immediacy in the process creates this illusion of liveness.
  \cite{raley2009list}

%Resaltar la relacion humano maquina, porque el feedback es importante 
Live coding is at the intersection of software art and performative arts, where the concept of "live" is embedded in the software itself and the performative act. In general, one of the fundamental principles of \textit{livecoding} performances is live improvisation through coding. This is achieved by creating and manipulating algorithms in real time to produce the visual and auditory experience for the audience. This can be done either from scratch or by modifying pre-existing code in response to the specific context of the performance. \textit{Soon Winnie} defines an algorithm as: “a more abstract term that is not dependent on any specific programming language or syntax but describes a step-by-step procedure used to achieve certain tasks through computation.” \cite{soon2018executing}  The manipulation and conception of algorithms within this performative practice allow the performer to bring their creative ideas to life, solve any problems that may arise, or propose new ideas on stage. Another key element within \textit{livecoding} is demonstrating to the audience that what they are witnessing is being created live. This is why the performer's screen is usually projected, revealing everything, including mistakes. 

  The first live coding performances emerged in the early 21st century, primarily within the contexts of electronic music and digital arts. 
The development of the practice was facilitated by programs focused on real-time audio processing. Software such as Miller Puckette’s Pure Data (developed in the 1990s) and James McCartney’s SuperCollider (originally released in 1996) could synthesise sound directly from algorithms. These programmes allow users to edit the code while the programme is running, enabling performative and improvisational experiences.
 \cite{usermanual}

% At its core, live coding is a form of real-time improvisation that uses programming as a creative medium. \cite{usermanual} 

The feedback loop between the artist and the program is a crucial element that allows for liveness in live coding performances. Artist and programmer  \textit{Alex McLean} identifies three main types of feedback in the experience of a live coding performance. \textit{Manipulation feedback},
 which occurs between the programmer and their written code. \textit{Performance feedback}, which occurs between the 
 programmer and the program output, such as sound. \textit{Social feedback}, which occurs between the audience and the programmer. \cite{mclean2011artist} \textit{McLean} expands on the concept of feedback, considering the elements surrounding the performance experience, such as the audience or sound and images generated by the performer. One implication of constant feedback between these elements is that they are constantly communicating with each other, so each element can affect or be affected by the experience or another element present in the performance. For example, the energy of the audience can influence the performer's decisions about the direction of the performance. Alternatively, a technical difficulty within the programme may require the performer to solve the problem quickly in order to maintain the performance's flow as much as possible, potentially changing some of the previously planned elements.

 %These actions are then processed and a request and feedback are sent within the framework of how this tool is

Another key factor that has shaped \textit{livecoding} as we know it today is that programming in contemporary software engineering has been reduced to a single phase. In \textit{livecoding} performances, different processes occur at the same time, with several commands running at once and constant code editing. Computer science scholar \textit{Steven L. Tanimoto} highlights the shift in computer programming from an edit-compile-link-run cycle to a single phase. Code runs continuously, even with various on-the-fly code edits. Additionally, \textit{Tanimoto} explains that the program must also be intuitive, enabling the programmer to understand what the program is doing and the next steps in the process \cite{tanimoto1990viva}. In this sense, liveness is based on the near-instant feedback between a programme and a programmer, and on the ability to run several processes simultaneously. This change that \textit{Tanimoto} mentions, where the programming processes are synthesised into one phase, is essential for maintaining a fluid performance in \textit{livecoding} performances. At the same time, it is better for the performer to have a simple notation system that enables them to respond quickly by typing new code in response to new demands or changes on stage. 

\textit{Livecoding} builds on the legacy of software art and the technical developments of software engineering. These two disciplines have made this performative practice and its search for liveness possible. Practitioners often have scientific and/or artistic backgrounds, resulting in a diverse range of tools and artistic approaches being developed, conceptualised and studied by the \textit{livecoding} community.

\subsection{The grammar of performative coding}

%Now, I would like to mention some key points in consolidating the philosophy of live coding. How is it that there is a practice around the action of programming on stage?  

Live coding embraces a philosophy in which processes are the protagonists of the
performative act. Here, being able to see the process unfold, with a set of instructions
executed simultaneously, is also a way to see the artist's ideas and singularities.
There is a communication process between the interfaces, the performer, and the
public. The concept of liveness involves exploring the expressivity of coding while avoiding
a mechanical approach. Due to its openness, this concept has become political,
allowing one to modify the software and inviting new collectives to form. 

This practice operates at the intersection of the technical and artistic domains. Software
development has been an essential element in the evolution of this practice, and it
has enabled all the artistic experimentation and theorization. In live coding, the concept of users disappears. The practitioners are involved in the
creation, maintenance, and conceptualization of the software. The division between
the final product and the user cannot apply. This is the key point for diversity of
approaches within the practice. It allows for different kinds of disciplinary intersections.

Two key artists who helped establish live coding as a practice in the software and media art scene were software artist and musician \textit{Adrian Ward} and programmer and artist \textit{Alex McLean}. They became collaborators in the early 2000s while studying at the \textit{University of Plymouth} in the UK. They shared the same interest and desire for programming to be a field of creativity and exploration. At that time, in the early 2000s, \textit{Ward} won the Software Art Prize at \textit{Transmediale} in 2001 for his project \textit{AutoIllustrator} (2001), a semi-autonomous parody of \textit{Adobe Illustrator} that generates visuals and challenges notions of programmable creativity. The following year, \textit{McLean} won the same prize for \textit{Forkbomb.pl} (2002), a simple script that visualizes crashing the machine on which it is run.

%podrias hacer pies de pagina de cada artista mencionando sus trabajos más importantes aparte del que ya esta en el texto


\textit{Ward} and \textit{McLean} explored the relationship between packaged and improvised software, which is why they were interested in creating their own software instead of using commercial, ready-to-use software. These explorations led to the formation of \textit{Slub}, an experimental live coding group founded in 2000. \textit{Slub} made their music exclusively from their own generative software and projected their screens so their audience could see their handmade interfaces. In September 2000, they presented \textit{The Generative Manifesto} at a \textit{Perl} community event held at the  \textit{Institute of Contemporary Arts (ICA)} in London. During the presentation, \textit{Ward} recited the manifesto while \textit{McLean} played a soundtrack of synthesized rhythms.
\cite{history} This manifesto establishes the principles and guidelines of their generative software practice and, at the same time, outlines key points in the practice of live coding, such as real-time output, code as an expressive medium, and the importance of transparency in processes. 

\textit{The Generative Manifesto} was the seed of later explorations. Over the years, more artists and developers embraced the idea of using the laptop as an expressive interface and worked to build environments that allow real-time output.\footnote{Another important factor that made live coding possible as we know it today was that laptops with powerful processors (CPUs), adequate memory (RAM), and capable graphics chips became more affordable for individuals. This made it possible for performers to start using laptops as creative tools in clubs and cultural venues} For example, five years later, \textit{McLean} developed \textit{Tidal Cycles}, a domain-specific language \footnote{Domain-Specific Languages (DSLs) are computer languages that are designed for specific applications} focused on generating and manipulating audiovisual patterns, incorporating some of the ideas from this early manifesto. The points from the Generative Manifesto were as follows:

\begin{adjustwidth}{1.27cm}{1.27cm}
\begin{itemize}
   \renewcommand{\labelitemi}{--}
  \item  Attention to detail—that only hand-made generative music can allow (code allows you to go deeper into creative structures);
 \item Realtime output and compositional control—we hate to wait (it is inconceivable to expect non-realtime systems to exhibit signs of life);
  \item  Construct and explore new sonic environments with echoes from our own. (art reflects human narrative, code reflects human activity);
\item Open process, open minds—we have nothing to hide (code is unambiguous, it can never hide behind obscurity. We seek to abolish obscurity in the arts);
\item  Only use software applications written by ourselves—software dictates output, we dictate software (authorship cannot be granted to those who have not authored!)

\cite{generativeManifesto}
\end{itemize}

\end{adjustwidth}


From this \textit{Generative Manifesto}, it is important to note how certain clear principles are established regarding the software used. Real time is directly linked to the liveness of software and compositional processes. The design of creative tools is emphasized, embracing the do-it-yourself philosophy. The tools used must respond to the specific needs of the artist, not the other way around, where the artist must adapt to and constrain an external design. There is also an emphasis on openness, which involves being clear and transparent about processes, showing the public what the artist is building on stage.

In February 2004, the \textit{Changing Grammars} symposium was convened by artist \textit{Renate Wieser} and musician \textit{Julian Rohrhuber} at the \textit{HfbK (Hochschule für Bildende Künste Hamburg)}. The symposium provided an opportunity for live coders and artists to discuss their practice and form a community around it.  The festival was politically engaged, concerned with code as art and live audio programming, and focused on open-source as a form of collective thinking. The festival's description reads, "Some computer languages allow changing a running process on the fly by rewriting the code that defines it. Applied to computer music, this means one can write sound compositions while they are playing." \cite{grammars}
 During this symposium, various collaborative live coding practices, as well as workshops and talks, were carried out, focusing on the writing of code as a possibility for musical composition. Among the live coders who participated in this festival were \textit{Adrian Ward}, \textit{Alex McLean}, \textit{Alberto de Campo}, and \textit{Nick Collins}.

The \textit{Changing Grammar Symposium} also provided an opportunity to network and organize around live coding. \textit{Toplap} was formed during the symposium. The name is a play on words, switching the order of "laptop." The collective's full name is \textit{The Temporary Organization for the Promotion of Live Algorithm Programming}. At that time, the collective's main objectives were to create a public archive and connect artists and people interested in live coding. The collective's first actions were to create a wiki and a mailing list. They also started drafting the collective's manifesto, which is still a draft open to modifications. The manifesto served as a guide for creating methodologies within the practice and for establishing important principles to discuss at that time. 

These are the original points of the \textit{Toplap Manifesto}:


\begin{adjustwidth}{1.27cm}{1.27cm}
We demand:
\begin{itemize}
   \renewcommand{\labelitemi}{--}
  \item Give us access to the performer's mind, to the whole human instrument.
  \item Obscurantism is dangerous. Show us your screens
  \item Programs are instruments that can change themselves
  \item Code should be seen as well as heard, underlying algorithms viewed as well as their visual outcome.
  \item  Live coding is not about tools. Algorithms are thoughts. Chainsaws are tools. That’s why algorithms are sometimes harder to notice than chainsaws.
\end{itemize} 
We recognise continuums of interaction and profundity, but prefer: 
\begin{itemize}
   \renewcommand{\labelitemi}{--}
  \item Insight into algorithms
    \item  The skillful extemporisation of algorithm as an expressive/impressive display of mental dexterity
    \item No backup (minidisc, DVD, safety net computer)
\end{itemize} 
 We acknowledge that: 
\begin{itemize}
   \renewcommand{\labelitemi}{--}
  \item    It is not necessary for a lay audience to understand the code to appreciate it, much as it is not necessary to know how to play guitar in order to appreciate watching a guitar performance.
   \item  Live coding may be accompanied by an impressive display of manual dexterity and the glorification of the typing interface.
    \item Performance involves continuums of interaction, covering perhaps the scope of controls with respect to the parameter space of the artwork, or gestural content, particularly directness of expressive detail. Whilst the traditional haptic rate timing deviations of expressivity in instrumental music are not approximated in code, why repeat the past? No doubt the writing of code and expression of thought will develop its own nuances and customs.
\end{itemize} 
\cite{history}
\end{adjustwidth}

In the \textit{Toplap Manifesto}, we see that the process plays a privileged role in live coding, and that the call for transparency is also political. From there, the idea of starting from scratch became more prevalent as a form of expression. There is also a demand for the performative experience to be completely ephemeral and for no backups of the code to be made.

Currently, \textit{Toplap} is an umbrella organization that brings together numerous artists and collectives. It connects projects from around the world. Although there are numerous nodes in different countries that are connected to each other, they have developed their own approaches to live coding. The community has grown and undertaken major initiatives, such as the \textit{International Conference on Live Coding (ICLC}), which began in 2015 at the \textit{University of Leeds} in the United Kingdom. Since then, the conference has been held annually or biennially, depending on the needs of the organization in each location. The conference is organized by various entities, including universities and collectives interested in researching live coding. During the conference, numerous concerts, workshops, and talks are held, and it also serves as a networking and knowledge-sharing space. Each conference edition has different thematic focuses. For example, the \textit{ICLC 2025} theme was \textit{liveness} along with some session topics such as: \textit{Specialized Tools and DSLs}, \textit{Live Coding Beyond Computers and Interfaces, Audio-Visual Live Coding, Speculative Futures, Communities and Artistic Practices}, and \textit{Collaborative Live Coding.} These topics reflect on liveness and its many forms and consequences within live coding, addressing the tension between what is live and what is not.  Taking into consideration the different dimensions in which live coding develops, such as its performative character, the construction of collective and community, and its different hybridizations and intersections with other practices

The \textit{Generative Manifesto} and \textit{Toplap} have shaped and established certain structures that have significantly favored the development of live coding. For example, the idea of using self-created software has generated numerous live coding environments with different functionalities and approaches. When writing their own software, artists can more efficiently address their own interests and needs. The idea of transparency and openness has led to certain aesthetic and methodological choices, such as projecting the code and making it visible to the public, as well as starting from scratch. Prioritizing expressive algorithmic processes over final products leads to embracing error. The concept of a score shifts, and the compositional process takes place during the performance, so musical or visual pieces do not correspond to a representation and are the result of improvisation based on the ideas the performer develops on stage. The liveliness also resides in the software's ability to provide real-time output of the actions performed by the artist, and in the artist interacting dynamically with the software during the performance, giving protagonism to the conversation generated between the interface and the artist.


\subsection{Programming livenness}


Live coding employs \textit{Domain-Specific Languages (DSLs)}, defined as computer languages that are designed for specific applications. In the context of live coding, the objective of these languages is to enhance the performative aspect by facilitating more intuitive and expressive coding. In the domain of sound live coding, for instance, these languages possess the capacity to modify and combine incoming audio streams and/or stored samples through the written execution of lines of code that are readily comprehensible to individuals with a background in sound and music. 
An illustrative example of this would be the naming of effects after sound-related terminology. This intuitive nature is particularly important for artists from non-programming or coding backgrounds.
\cite{puckette2007theory}
\footnote{For example, in the case of a distortion effect within a livecoding environment, the term 'distort' would most likely be used while coding.}

In contradistinction to general-purpose programming, livecoding environments are oriented towards the specific domains of audio and visual patterns, with the purpose of creating and modifying performances in real time.\cite{Embed}
It is also important to note that the notation employed in each environment is subject to variation, depending on the specific conceptual framework and programming languages that are used. For instance, \textit{Hydra} is a live coding environment for visuals that draws inspiration from analogue modular synthesizers. Using \textit{JavaScript}, it is browser-based. The notation system focuses on terminology related to visual representations. Functions such as \textit{.shape()} and \textit{.color()} have been employed to provide insight into their use. These functions are characterised by their interdependent sequence, meaning that the output of one process becomes the input of the next, as is the case with patching a physical synthesizer.\cite{jack2019hydra}

A number of video synthesiser prototypes inspired by music synthesizers were developed in the late 1960s and 1970s. 
For instance, in 1968, \textit{Eric Siegel}, a pioneer in the field, started working on the \textit{Processing Chrominance Synthesizer (PCS)}.

When using an analog synthesizer, you have to plug in a source, which can be a shape, oscillation, or an external video source. Then, you can try effects like waveshapers and RGB signals. There's another way to alter the video signals: create feedback using your output as a source. You can also achieve this type of effects and feedback with \textit{Hydra}. At the \textit{Tina Frank´s} class I experimented with various modulations, ramps, and feedback patches on the analogue video synthesizer (Figure 5). This helped me also to understand the operations I perform with \textit{Hydra} in a more tactile way.\footnote{You can see a video of a patch I made in the Visual Synth class here: \includegraphics[height=20mm]{synthcode.png}}

Let me explain in more detail how this feedback would in \textit{Hydra} notation. Take the following piece of code, for example:
\[osc(10).thresh().invert(() => Math.sin(time)).out(o0)\]  
\textit{osc()} stands for oscillation, which is the source. In the middle are \textit{thresh()} and \textit{invert()}, which affect the source. The last thing is the output, \textit{out().}\footnote{You can try out this code in the hydra web \href{https://hydra.ojack.xyz}https://hydra.ojack.xyz}

Now, I will take a modulation. Here, you must select a source again. It can be a shape or an oscillator. Here, however, we will use our output \textit{o0} to create feedback and we placed it as \textit{modulate(o0)}.
\[osc(10).thresh().modulate(o0).invert(() => Math.sin(time)).out(o0)\]

If you try the code on the \textit{Hydra} website, you'll see that this small change of routing the output inside a modulation creates a loop that generates patterns like trails. Other results that can be achieved using feedback include fractals or infinite zooms.

Another important live coding environment that is worth mentioning is \textit{Tidal Cycles}, which was developed by \textit{Alex McLean} during his PhD at \textit{Goldsmiths, University of London},

It uses \textit{SuperDirt} as a framework to function as a sampler, and \textit{SuperCollider} to provide

but the structure is cyclical, not linear (Figure 6). One cycle follows another. This idea is especially good in some types of electronic music, 
which are based on constant repetition. The program's library includes various drum machines, synthesizers, 
and bass sounds that are commonly used in this genre.

These projects include research on choreography in digital spaces, experimental literary structures, and navigable, three-dimensional, multi-user spaces on the internet.

This project incorporates the body into the creative process in a web environment. When you open the \textit{Hydra} pop-up window, the webcam activates by default.  This work explores the concept of the body and the choreographic possibilities that can be explored in web spaces, as well as their relationship to live coding as a form of writing that activates different meanings and physical signs. \textit{Joana Chicau} has conducted research on choreographic processes and their relationship to live coding. thinking has been a way to bridge and enhance the somatic and semantic within coding. \cite{usermanual} \end{adjustwidth} 
 
In \textit{ Algorithmic Artscape}, the act of coding involves declaring and executing actions, where the presence of the body becomes more evident through different strategies within the webpage. The choreography here becomes a dynamic process of creation that, instead of being fixed, lends itself to improvisation, taking the different signs and inputs that the website itself provides. This work brings together choreography and digital spaces, offering a more physical relationship between humans and machines. This communication is no longer conveyed only through different programming languages, but also through the various gestures and movements that this interface can activate in the body.


Another notable project is the \textit{Betablocker} live-coding system, which was introduced in a 2006 study by artist and scientist \textit{Till Bovermann} and researcher and game designer \textit{Dave Griffiths}. It combines live coding with computational processing. As \textit{Boverman} and \textit{Griffiths} state in their investigation(2014), \textit{Betablocker} brings to light many different strategies for presenting computation as tangible material by making computational processing as much a part of live coding as the code that describes it. \textit{Betablocker} features an imaginary central processing unit architecture designed specifically for live coding. It uses a byte code-level visualization and an icon-based assembler (Figure 8). The primary design focus of \textit{Betablocker} allows for easy modifications, enabling introspection by adding probes and customization by altering the instruction set. It is designed to be controlled by a gamepad, using the \textit{Fluxus} gaming engine. This translates the traditional way of using live coding, which is to create and modify code by typing on a keyboard.
\cite{bovermann2014computation}

When \textit{Betablocker} is implemented on the stage, different blocks can be connected to create code and processes that modify and destroy each other within 256 bytes of memory. The machine's memory and processes are integral to the performance and these are projected to the public. This software allows you to visualize computational processes in a playful way while they are sonically translated. However, this is not evident to an audience unfamiliar with the conventions and set of instructions, which can lead to wide interpretations. On stage, a grid of blocks with different designs appears and disappears. In any case, from the perspective of an audience unfamiliar with the project, it can be inferred that the blocks that interact with each other and that the performance connects and disconnects are creating the audio input. In terms of its visual design and execution, \textit{Betablocker} is closer to the concept of an \textit{esoteric programming language} \footnote{For example, one can consider the \textit{esoteric programming language Piet}, which resembles the visual representation of \textit{Betablocker}. This language generates bitmaps resembling abstract paintings by \textit{Piet Mondrian}, and the code is executed by moving the cursor across a grid.}. These are unusual or weird languages in which the visual or conceptual aspect often takes prominence over the functional aspect.

\textit{Betablocker} challenges the traditional concept of a written programming language. Here, we encounter a visual language that employs graphic conventions associated with the computational processes of an imaginary computer. \textit{Betablocker} then assigns a sound representation to these processes. For instance, the computer's performance influences the audio output. If the memory becomes overloaded, some processes will terminate, affecting the sound. Another interesting aspect is how the performer interacts with the software: by connecting different blocks to create sound and by moving within a grid of blocks to create or remove them. This places improvisation in the playful aspect of the software rather than in the performer's ability to type code on stage.

\textit{VERSNIZ} implements IBNIZ as a live coding component that serves as the basis for creating various visual and audio elements within this immersive, multi-user environment. IBNIZ, which stands for 'Ideally Bare Numeric Impression Gizmo', was created by developer and artist \textit{Ville-Matias Heikkilä}(2012).\cite{ibniz2} This programming language generates video and audio from very short strings of code and can be categorized as an esoteric programming language due to its minimalism. Since the creation of IBNIZ, various musical experiments have emerged from the use of this live coding language. Some time later, the term \textit{bytebeat} was coined to describe an experimental type of music made from (typically short) mathematical formulas that define waveforms as a function of time.\cite{ibniz} This genre is related to others such as chiptune or 8-bit. The use of virtual keyboards in VR is often limited to short texts due to the difficulty of using them with the available input devices. For this reason, IBNIZ is a good choice for a performing experience in this type of VR environment (Figure 9).\cite{ver}.


In the \textit{VERSNIZ} environment, live coding objects can be moved and modified in space. This allows multiple objects to interact during the performance. These live coding objects enable the creation of visual forms, animation, and music. This composes both the space and the performative experience.\cite{ver} Creating a performance in this type of virtual environment makes it possible to hold events in physical places and in the virtual realm. \textit{VERSNIZ} is an innovative project that makes use of immersive VR environments, allowing not only for audiovisual output to be considered, but also for the construction of the performance space itself, which is only possible in this type of virtual environment.


As mentioned in the previous projects, different strategies have been employed in which code is embedded in interfaces that challenge the role of the body in computer-based performances. For example, in \textit{Algorithm Artscape}, several programming languages interpellate the performer to create different gestures with the body and with the code. This means that the role of the body is not limited to moving the cursor on the screen, but also involves a gestural and physical interaction with the interface that expands it. In the case of \textit{VERSNIZ}, the body, when in a VR environment, becomes an extension of itself in this immersive reality. The body is contained within a virtual space that can be explored in different directions and which is created and modified by 3D objects and live coding components. This allows both the performer and the audience to inhabit this virtual space, either within the VR environment with the headset or through a projection in which one can observe how the performer creates their space.

These projects aim to create more experimental programming languages and more dynamic ways to activate them. This approach offers varied ways of conceiving code and performance, combining elements of software development with elements of live arts, sound and visual arts. The development of these types of projects generates diverse research in which code is not necessarily linked to the optimisation of programs, allowing different intersections between disciplines and collaborations between artists, developers and scientists who want to explore the different facets that technology can have from a sensitive approach. For example, computational processes can be transferred to a set of conventions that are then translated into audio, which can generate different sound compositions, as we saw in the Betablocker project. These operations generate expressiveness in technological elements to which a creative role is not usually assigned.  These collaborative and open investigations counterbalance many technology industries where the development of devices and programs is carried out with a high degree of secrecy and control over the results. The mentioned projects demonstrate a greater degree of uncertainty and openness to reappropriations and collaborations, such as the VERSNIZ project, which uses IBNIZ, an earlier livecoding language, to create its implementation in the VR world. These types of operations demonstrate an active community that builds on existing research to generate new interpretations and create and explore in various directions, allowing autonomy regarding the tools involved.

When conceptualising the performance, I took the sounds of a hyper-technological society as my starting point: noise, the incessant hum of construction, machines that shake the ground, the saturation of cities and silence, the decline or absence of other species. For this performance, I was influenced by the work of \textit{Bernie Krause} on biophony and soundscapes. 
In the late 1960s, he began researching and documenting the sounds of natural ecosystems around the world. 
His archive includes ecosystems that have since been wiped out by human activity. 
He has collected over 5,000 hours of recordings from natural environments, 
including at least 15,000 terrestrial and marine species from around the world \cite{ribac2016bernie}. Krause coined the term \textit{Animal Orchestra} to describe the sonic diversity of different species, which produce sounds across a wide range of frequencies and pitches, much like the interplay of various musical instruments in an orchestra (figure 11). Krause and scientist Stuart Gage developed the acoustic niche hypothesis, which posits that living beings that share an ecosystem
 evolve to produce sounds with different rhythms and tones so as not to interfere with each other \cite{krause2016wild}. 
 This is important for finding mates, food, and water.
  It is a harmonious system in which different species coexist alongside their diverse sounds, which are astonishingly beautiful. 

Listening to \textit{Bernie's} early recordings from the 1970s instantly evokes a sense of nostalgia for a distant,
  highly diverse world that is far removed from city life. Although many ecosystems remain intact, many are under threat.  The sound of cars or air traffic makes it difficult for many species to hear each other, leaving them vulnerable to predators or making it difficult for them to mate. And as for us, our noise pollution is often unbearable. While cities may have an interesting soundscape, rapid growth or change leads to noise becoming predominant.


\textit{Krause} made his first sound recording of \textit{Sugarloaf State Park} in \textit{Sonoma Valley, California}, 
with the intention of documenting the environment near his home. From then on, he regularly 
recorded that park. In 2011, the area suffered a severe drought that lasted for years.
 As can be seen in Figure 12 , the spectrogram shows how the soundscape gradually falls silent: 
 birds no longer sing as they once did because they have to conserve energy due to food shortages,
  while the insect population declines and the vegetation dries up. Finally, in 2017, 
  the forest fire broke out, affecting much of the area.

Silence is also a homogeneous mass of noise. In \textit{Dusk of an Electric Forest}, I explored the idea that silence is not an absence of sound or a pause, but an overlapping mass of noise. To achieve this, I recorded sounds from construction sites in the city, such as the noise of drills, the movement of heavy machinery across asphalt, and car alarms. I also recorded the \textit{Traun River} flowing and insects singing as a counterbalance to the urban sounds. The \textit{Traun River} is a body of water located less than 10 minutes by train from the city of Linz. It is an area with plenty of vegetation, but it also has a hydroelectric plant in its waters. It is a river with a strong contrast between nature and industry, which is why it caught my attention as a sound source.

During the performance, moments of unintelligible, 
grainy murmurs can be heard, which then become more rhythmic and repetitive. The performance begins with the sounds of insects moving from one source to another in stereo. Samples of drills and hammers are mixed with the continuous murmur of water. The visuals feature a glitch grid that resembles cartographies of saturated colours (figure 13).
As the performance progresses, these small squares change position and colour, and the textures rotate and become increasingly grainy,

Within the \textit{Hydra} environment, I modified this sequence using code, which allowed me to create modulations and experiment with time, textures and colours.

For this performance, I primarily used samples from the \textit{SuperCollider Tidal Cycles} sample library. This library contains sounds from iconic drum machines, such as the \textit{Roland TR-808} and \textit{TR-909}, which I used in \textit{City Landscapes}. I created a sequence of drum rhythms, a fast bass line and kicks with reverb, as well as claps and snare. I recorded myself saying short phrases, divided them into small parts, sped them up and randomised them to fit the composition.
It was minimalist techno, retaining the essential elements to make people dance and creating some polyrhythms so that it wasn't monotonous.

In the image, you can see how the previous frame freezes in the visuals and remains on the screen for a few seconds, scrolling sideways, and also how the image blurs in diferent directions.

In this performance, improvisation did not start from scratch: sounds and images had been curated in advance, and the potential course of the performance had been planned and rehearsed. However, there was room for error, and I did not intend to be entirely governed by a strict plan and schedule. Although we can consider the computer and live coding to be contemporary musical instruments, the concept of a musical score becomes blurred in this context.

\subsection{Toplap Linz}

In June 2024, I had the opportunity to teach a workshop on \textit{Tidal Cycles} with the \textit{NoiseMeetup} collective. 
This was my first time teaching a workshop on this tool, and I invited \textit{Gorka Egino} to collaborate with me. 
We used \textit{Flok.cc} to connect all the participants to the same platform. First, we provided an introduction, covering how the tool works, how to create initial sounds and how to generate compositions using code. The second part consisted of playing together using \textit{Flok}. From that first experience, we learned that in order to start a jam, certain rules must
 be established, as having more than 15 people play together on the same interface 
 can quickly lead to chaos. This workshop also led to the creation of \textit{Toplap Linz}.

Since \textit{Toplap Linz} is a \textit{Toplap} node located in Linz, Austria. It was founded on 2024 as part of the broader international \textit{Toplap} community to explore and promote live coding practices. It is aligned with the open-source philosophy and one of its core pillars are being inclusive and accessible. As a result of the creation of \textit{Toplap Linz}, we also started organising meetings to answer questions, share knowledge and try new things. However, these meetings were not as well received, so after a few attempts, we began to wonder if our reach was limited, or if the invitations were ambiguous or unappealing.

Some time later, in collaboration with \textit{Noise Meetup}, we held a second two-day workshop. We repeated the workshop and jam format, and this time, as well as teaching \textit{Tidal Cycles}, we included \textit{Hydra}. This event was held at DH5. We dedicated the first day to workshops and the second to a computer jam. This iteration attracted a significantly larger number of participants, who provided highly positive feedback, contributing to the overall success of the event - writing code and having fun by the end. 

This time, we made sure to install \textit{Tidal Cycles} on the computers of those attending for the first time, despite knowing it would be time-consuming and potentially cumbersome. We felt it was important for people to have the tool ready to use outside the workshop, in other contexts. We also wanted to provide a space for group installation, as this process can be confusing if you don't have experience with operating system command applications. Our goal was to make the experience as enjoyable as possible from the outset.

Then, we got down to business and shared a \textit{Flok.cc} session where everyone could connect at the same time and review the code.
 We also created a cheatsheet for \textit{Tidal Cycles} and \textit{Hydra} with guidelines to help participants start exploring the tool. 
 It's a simple guide offering an overview of the possibilities of these two environments 
 for experimenting with sound and visuals, respectively (see fig. 20).
From my experience of learning to use different software,
 I have personally found it quite useful to be able to follow the process on my own computer. 
 This allows you to interact more effectively with the tools when someone explains something in a masterclass.

On the second day of the workshop, we devoted ourselves solely to computer jam sessions. We timed each session, starting with five minutes, then moving on to ten, twenty and thirty minutes. In the longer sessions, we worked with themes. For instance, in one session, we used the symbol \textit{520}, which is used in China to say 'I love you'. The visuals and sound were often humorous and corny. In another session, someone suggested using horses. Interesting things happened in this dynamic and we were all able to collaborate. Sometimes, after a jam, questions arose about how to create a particular rhythm or apply an effect to the visuals. We realised that, in the jam dynamic, people dared to try more complex things.
 
As a general rule, we decided not to copy code from sources outside our \textit{Flok} page, such as the official website or online tutorials. There were two main reasons for this. The first is that it is difficult to predict the result of mixing pieces of code that are not understood — it may not work at all. The second is that if you join someone else's code to yours, it will most likely break and stop working. Therefore, we focused on the process of crafting the code, adopting a slower but more deliberate approach to ideation and elaboration. We always started from scratch and developed the jam together.

Another important principle that applies to any type of jam session is the importance of active listening. This involves understanding each other while doing something together and knowing when to try a sound from the library and how to introduce it. When you are unsure of the sound you want to introduce, it is often better not to go in at full volume. You could also choose to play divisions and rhythms similar to those of others, or simpler divisions, before moving on to polyrhythms.

In a group of more than six people improvising simultaneously, 
it is important to understand that there are times when it is better to give others space and remain silent. 
These pauses are useful for taking a step back and observing what is happening without the pressure of having to keep programming. They also give you a moment to think about how you can contribute something new to the jam, such as another sound, rhythm, modification of the existing code, image or effect.

In this thesis, I have traced a journey through experiences in the practice of live coding, exploring different methodologies for conceptualization and realization, and working with varying degrees of improvisation, planning, and execution. This has also been a personal journey to understand what brings a practice such as programming to life and what happens when it encounters the performative arts.



For me, live coding as a performative practice is a field of experimentation. I invite the audience to observe how I build what they are hearing and seeing, activating their gaze so they become witnesses to constant decision‑making. They can perceive doubt, errors, skill, and audacity as they emerge. In this context, the computer contains the body, and the body extends the computer. In this exercise of response and interaction, the body is not passive before the interface; it is the body that dynamically activates it.

I have used different live coding environments and explored their possibilities. For me, text-based notations are easier to understand and perform. I found this kind of notation easier to manipulate live. In any case, patcher languages like Max/MSP, which is a visual language, have been useful to me when I want to use long tracks or audio in a performance. This is something that is really hard to do in Strudel or Tidal Cycles because they are based on really tiny repetitions to create rhythms. These live coding environments address specific demands. For example, they need to be clear and concise so that you don't panic when writing complicated functions on stage. 

For me, Hydra has been one of the most fascinating environments in my practice. It's intuitive, and you can do a lot with simple things, but it can also get really complex if you want. You can embed Hydra in other environments, such as Strudel, P5.js, and Mercury, among others. This can be useful and practical for performative practices. Furthermore, it can be used for web design because it is easy to implement on web pages.   


In my own practice, there has been ambivalence in terms of score, choreography, and improvisation. In some cases, such as my performance Dusk of an Electric Forest, there is a concept and a prior curation of images and sounds, the code is constructed from an established frame, and there is more or less an idea of different moments in the performance. However, even in this type of experience, the performance is not limited to the mechanical act of activating and deactivating lines of code. It involves working with a limited color palette, but with several parameters that remain open and escape any planning. 

Although in Dusk of an Electric Forest, I had rehearsed different effects, rhythms, and patterns. When the moment of the performance arrived, things were different. A space of uncertainty opens up, and being flexible is the key to enjoying and constructing the performance. Things look and sound different on speakers than on headphones, or on a computer screen and a projector. The acoustic and visual conditions are totally different, which means you have to be able to adapt.

\subsection{Vanishing photographs }

%In my participation of \textit{Sankt interface} %define Sankt 

I participated in \textit{Sankt Interface 2024}, an art exhibition and celebration organizated by \textit{Interfaces Cultures}, in wich students can showcase projects based on the idea of subverting the mouse and keyboard interface. I did a camera in which the image is never stable and with even little movements will start to blur and vanish, in every click you freeze a different moment, but it will tend to disappear.

I thought for this work on the difficulties of archiving and how memory is something that we can not rely entirely. In a era where images are hyper abundant, we value then less, somethings the photos that we take re forgotten in  a cloud or even worse just existing in pur celphone with the threaten of disappear if something happen to that device.

I would like to reference \textit{Susan Sontag's} famous take on photography and its relationship with mortality and ephemerality: All photographs are memento mori. To take a photograph is to participate in another person’s (or thing’s) mortality, vulnerability, mutability. Precisely by slicing out this moment and freezing it, all photographs testify to time’s relentless melt.\cite{sontag2021photography}

For this project, I worked with a toy camera that I found on the street. It's a nostalgic object that was transformed into a mouse and regained its symbolic function of capturing images. This artifact is capable of freezing time and then also allows you to see how time resists being captured and the image finally vanish.

The computer is another element that acts as a mirror; one can only capture an image through their reflection. This narrows the possibilities of the artifact; the audience can only take self-portraits, which makes it more intimate. You don't just see how an image crumbles; you actually see yourself melting away.

On the computer, a \textit{JavaScript} code is running within the \textit{Hydra} web. There are two mouse interactions: the first freezes the image when clicked, and the second modulates the image when the mouse moves from side to side, producing the vanishing effect.
