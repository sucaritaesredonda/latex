\section{Abstract}

Live coding -as defined by \textit{Alex McLean}, one of the most relevant figures in the field- is the practice of constructing and
interacting with algorithms during a performance. It aims to convey algorithmic thinking, real-time composition and networked collaborations to the
audience.\cite{usermanual}
 A distinctive quality of improvisation is the temporal element, 
 which involves interacting with tools and the capacity to rethink rules and conditions.
  There is no concern on optimisation and efficiency -features that typically characterise the technology industry-, 
  and instead there is a focus on embracing error, experimentation and open processes rather than generating outcomes.

In this thesis, I will review different experiences I have had in relation to my own live coding practice. I want to share the methodologies I have learned and discovered along my journey as a live coder. I will delve into my research on time in live coding, its playful nature, and how the community plays a fundamental role in consolidating an open source and diverse practice that aims to explore the computer as an expressive interface.

In the first part, I will go into more depth on certain technical and theoretical aspects of live coding, as well as a brief overview of the history of live coding and the most recent contributions made by the community in terms of developing new tools, experiments, and connections between different disciplines such as cinema.

Then, in the second part, I will talk about different performances and conceptual and technical concepts, as well as my experience co-organizing Toplap Linz, a community dedicated to the exchange of knowledge and experimentation in live coding.

Finally, I would like to share the documentation of the performances, photographs, and videos,
 as well as an interactive website that includes the code for some of the performances, 
 where you can experience an excerpt of what happened and also access the code, allowing visitors 
 to the website to copy, use, modify, and perhaps understand it.

\subparagraph{Key words} Audio-Visual live coding, collaborative live coding, communities and artistic practices,
algorithmic thinking and real-time composition.

\section{Introduction}


My interest in programming was a coincidence. I had finished my bachelor's 
degree in fine arts and had mostly worked with "analog" techniques (which it means that computer art was something foreign to me).
 After my time at the art academy, I found myself with the terrifying freedom of no longer being in an institution. 
 It was at that moment that I began working with \textit{Juana Prieto}, and the two of us started 
 this journey into the field of creative coding.

We had done our undergraduate studies together and had formed a friendship since then.
 Juana had the idea to make a GIF movie for the web — the text would lead to new places, 
 a linear narrative that ultimately became a labyrinth of moving images and blinking text. 
 Neither of us had extensive knowledge of programming, but I joined the project because the idea presented itself as a challenge that combined things that intrigued me, like the relationship between image and text, where both are equally important.

We spent months working with the image and text archives we each had, merging narratives and creating 
labyrinths together. Those were months of discovery for both of us. We had received support
 from the Cinemateca of Bogotá and were working in the institution’s labs. For that project, we used Twine, which is a mix of HTML and its own CSS-like style. As the weeks went by and we read the documentation, we began using JavaScript within the program to add some logic and elements that made sense within the web aesthetic — like decision boxes or having the audience answer questions.

From that moment on, programming stopped being an unattainable mystery reserved for engineers and computer scientists. I began to explore the potential of this language used to communicate with machines. While we were at the Cinemateca in Bogotá, we came across Toplap Bogotá, a live coding community. RafroBeat (who is also a member of Toplap) was giving a workshop on Tidal Cycles at the time, which marked the beginning of my journey with code as a performative act.

The week after that workshop, \textit{Malitzin Cortés and Iván Abreu}, two Mexican artists who work with code, architecture,
 and expanded cinema, gave a workshop where we used film archives as a 
 base to create live coding performances. 
 That was my first experience performing in front of an audience, and the first time Juana and I performed together.

Together, we created the collective \textit{Vestidas y Alborotadas} (Clothed and Rowdy)
 and became active members of the Toplap community. We performed numerous times in Bogotá, Medellín, and Manizales. 
 Together, we questioned the presence of the body on stage. And from that question,
In each performance, we embodied a different monster through the design of our costumes, 
which were made from upcycled textiles. 

Our intention was always to make people dance with our fast-paced music, using samples from our own voices and the sounds of our surroundings. 
We used our archive of photos and videos to create visuals. With each performance, we offered the audience a glimpse into the world of a different monster, complete with its own sounds and images, creating a narrative around these fictional beings. 
We didn't want to be perceived as "the girls" on stage in a predominantly masculine space, so we chose to be scary, weird, and monstrous in each performance.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{vest.JPG}
  \caption{ \textit{Vestidas y Alborotadas} performing at \textit{Cinemateca} of Bogotá, 2022. Picture by: Gerson Polo}
\end{figure}

In each performance of \textit{Vestidas y Alborotadas}, as well as wearing monster costumes, we created a series of images and videos of these monsters posing in different ways or in different locations around the city, either walking or standing still. We also liked to incorporate city sounds into our performances. Through these, we aimed to extend the actions of the monsters we embodied on stage. These monsters were a composition completed by visual and sonic elements, as well as our presence as these beings.

When I arrived at the Kunstuniversität Linz, 
I found an exciting musical and artistic scene in the city. 
The art scene, both inside and outside the university, 
thrilled me with its openness the spaces and the community are 
genuinely willing to see and listen to what you do. Over time, 
together with Gorka Egino (also a student at Kunstuniversität Linz and a livecoder), and with the support of the Noise Meetup Lab, 
we decided to create a live coding platform in Linz called Toplap Linz. 
Through a series of workshops and jam sessions, 
we aimed to build a space for sharing knowledge and fostering community.

The creative or inventive capacity of coding resides in its  more human side\cite{GenerativeaRT},
 in contrast to the process-optimization mindset we usually associate with code. 
  Here writing code is not about debugging but about expression and performativity of algorithmic thought.
 While it is a language that requires precision to be executed, its potential to expand the written word is unique.

Live coding is a practice in which the programmer writes their own writing machine. These are open machines, supported by a community.
 There are always collaborations, rewritings, updates, and improvements. And as live coding, artist-programmer 
 Alex McLean states: ``A program is not programmed to be used later,
 but is used by programming it.''\cite{meaningLife}.

  But going back to the basics, what is live coding? As I will present it later 
  in more detail, one of the most common definitions is that it is a practice of programming in front of an audience, where the code is executed in real time, 
  and the performer interacts with the code as part of the performance.\cite{cats}
  This definition, however, does not encompass all the nuances and possibilities of this practice.
  
  We would have to go deeper into the unfolding of the code within the performative act,
  the characterization of some Domain Specific Languages (DSLs), the playfulness within the scene,
  and the community that has been built around this practice, 
  and its open source philosophy. Liveness has more to do with making a political statement than with meeting a technical requirement.

 This thesis explores the concept of \textit{liveness} in live coding, whether in solo or collaborative performances, on-site or online, and how the code is written and executed in real time. In this work, I will discuss my experience of live coding, my involvement in toplap Linz as a co-founder, and the various methodologies and performances I have encountered on this journey. Some of the questions I want to address are: How does time exist in this on-the-fly programming?
 How do invention and intervention lead to playfulness? Why is it important to keep the code alive, and how does this approach become a political statement?

\section{Coding liveness}

This chapter focuses on providing an overview of live coding and what it
means to keep code "alive" from technical and theoretical perspectives.
The first section will bring some historical overview about software art and liveness within the context of coding art.
The second section will explore theoretical approaches to live coding that have shaped the practice, such as manifestos. Finally, the third section will introduce the some relevant DSLs (domain-specific languages) within my practice and the Linz community of live coding and computer music. 


\subsection{Software art }


In order to understand live coding, it is first necessary to discuss software art and its evolution, as these two disciplines are closely related.  This artistic approach considers software beyond its practical tool definition, shifting the focus to the expressiveness of the medium itself. Works of art explore the materiality of software and computer networks. As media theorist Tilman Baumgärtel states:

“Software art is not art that has been created with the help of a
computer but art that happens in the computer. Software is not
programmed by artists, in order to produce autonomous work,
but the software itself is the artwork. What is crucial here is not
the result but the process triggered in the computer by the
program  code"  \cite{baumgartel2001experimentelle}.

The word "software" first appeared in an exhibition called Software: Information Technology: Its New Meaning for Art," curated by artist and critic Jack Burnham, at the Jewish Museum in New York (1970–1971). The exhibition took software out of the field of engineering and emphasized its relationship to systems, data, and processes in the representation of artistic expressions and ideas.
\cite{katz1970software}

Another example of how software art began to gain more presence in festivals and exhibitions was when Transmediale introduced "artistic software" as a new category in 2001.  Also, in 2003, Ars Electronica, one of the biggest digital art festivals, focused its attention on code under the theme of “Code—The Language of Our Time.” This theme was approached as both artistic and political expression, reflecting on topics such as privacy and aesthetics related to software. 
\cite{broeckmann2006software}

Artist-coder and researcher Soon Winnie described the characteristics of 
software art as "rules, languages, programmability, and the live conditions of execution processes" \cite{soon2018executing}. 
These characteristics are also the main materialities that have been subjects of artistic experimentation, analysis from a sociological perspective, as in digital humanities, or technological development in the field of engineering. In the artistic field, software is not only a tool for producing autonomous works of art or expressions, but it is also "using code as an expression itself" (Andersen  Pold, 2004b).
\cite{andersen2004software}

The Book After the Book is a net art piece that reflects on the expressiveness of code and
 the transformations of literature in the context of the internet. It was published by Brazilian media 
 artist Giselle Beiguelman in 1999.  This work of art is a "new reading machine" in which knowledge is not 
 organized as it was in the past, when librarians classified and created categories to arrange books within a 
 physical space. Rather, this space functions "as a node in a network." In this webpage, one can wander through
  hyperlinks to different bookshelves that compile lists of literary or philosophical texts, net art, and, 
  in turn, it is a self-referential work that considers its own existence and its role in digital spaces. 
\cite{oliver2019digital}
  
In the section "Frictions" of The Book After the Book, you can read this statement about the text behind the webpage, questioning its aesthetic nature and the specifics of this language hidden in the backend of this digital medium:
\cite{beiguelman2019museums}

“Deep down, there on the back of the page, at the source, a situation is defined: the Internet
 is no more than a big text. But what happens when this text emerges, when it occupies the surface of the screen? 
 A strange passage is imposed: from screen to screen, the letter migrates, 
 it de-contextualises itself, posing language as an aesthetic problem. Given that, one asks: is this literature or not?”  
\cite{beiguelman2019museums}

 Beiguelman addresses the different computational layers in which code is present, being invisible as written instructions but giving life to the functioning of the web. It also asks about the materiality of the code itself, its aesthetic dimensions, and the reading act in digital and hybrid spaces beyond the printed medium.

 Some artists in the field of software art have become interested in the concept of liveness, 
 making use of "real-time computation, live data, and dynamic systems" \cite{soon2018executing}.  One work that uses real-time computation and takes the Internet Network as its main source is Listening Post (2000-2001) 
 by statistician Mark Hansen and sound artist Ben Rubin. In this work, more than 200 small screens show fragments of text from thousands of Internet chat rooms, bulletin boards, and public forums. The text sequence begins with the phrases "I am," "I like," or "I love."  A voice synthesizer reads some of the texts appearing on the screens simultaneously.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{listening.jpeg}
  \caption{ Listening Post by \textit{Mark Hansen and Ben Rubin} 
  (2000-2001). Picture by: \href{https://webarchive.ars.electronica.art/en/archives/prix_archive/prix_projekt.asp?iProjectID=12850.html}{Ars Electronica Archive}}
\end{figure}

 In this work, we encounter a system that operates autonomously. The running machine does not require human intervention 
 and creates a random composition of the data it incessantly collects.  
 Performance studies scholar Philip Auslander asks, "If Listening Post is a performance, who or what is the performer? 
 (…) Do machines perform?". \cite{auslander2005listening} This question is important for considering liveness beyond the human experience. It makes us consider technological processes that are hidden behind the work and its visual and sonic representation, such as data transmission, networking, and processing.

In the context of media art and technology, Auslander defined "live" as a representation in which the feedback 
given by the system or machine is almost immediate.
 "Live is actually an effect of mediatization [...] it was the development of recording technologies that 
 made it possible to perceive existing representation as live" \cite{auslander2022liveness}.  
 For example, in the case of Listening Post, we have a multimedia representation of a source of dynamic data 
 (in this case, the flow of text collected from different sites on the internet). 
 Scholar Rita Raley discusses Listening Post, noting that this installation captures the sentiment of the virtual crowd with information and voices in constant change, with approximately 100,000 real-time messages. She points out that, even if there is actually a delay of one or two hours (meaning it is not entirely real-time), the sensation of immediacy in the process creates this illusion of liveness.
  \cite{raley2009list}

Media studies scholar Jay David Bolter points out that procedurality is crucial to maintaining the feedback loop
 between humans and machines. For instance, consider a search engine that receives input from a human user. 
 These actions are then processed and a request and feedback are sent within the framework of how this tool is 
 programmed.  Bolter refers to these processes that maintain the feedback loop between humans and machines as the 
     \textit{performativity of digital media}. \cite{ekman2016ubiquitous}

In live coding, this feedback loop is essential to the performance. At its core, live coding is a form of real-time improvisation that uses programming 
as a creative medium. \cite{usermanual}  Artist and programmer Alex McLean highlights that the feedback relations between the program and performer are one 
of the key components when discussing the notion of liveness in live coding. He also identifies three main types 
of feedback in the experience of a live coding performance. "Manipulation feedback,"
 which occurs between the programmer and their written code. "Performance feedback," which occurs between the 
 programmer and the program output, such as sound. "Social feedback," which occurs between the audience and the
  programmer. \cite{mclean2011artist}


  The first live coding performances emerged in the early 21st century, primarily within the contexts of electronic music and digital arts. 
The development of the practice was facilitated by programs focused on real-time audio processing. Software such as Miller Puckette’s Pure Data (developed in the 1990s) and James McCartney’s SuperCollider (originally released in 1996) could synthesise sound directly from algorithms. These programmes allow users to edit the code while the programme is running, enabling performative and improvisational experiences.
 \cite{usermanual}


 In live coding performances, different processes occur simultaneously, with several commands running at once and constant code editing. This is because the programming process is reduced to a single phase. Computer science scholar Steven L. Tanimoto highlights the shift in computer programming from an edit-compile-link-run cycle to a single phase. Code runs continuously, even with various on-the-fly code edits. Additionally, Tanimoto explains that the program must also be intuitive, enabling the programmer to understand what the program is doing and the next steps in the process \cite{tanimoto1990viva}. In this sense, liveness is based on the near-instant feedback between a programme and a programmer, and on the ability to run several processes simultaneously.
 As Tanimoto mentioned, it is necessary to have intuitive programmes. For the live coder, for example, it is better to have a simple notation system that allows them to respond quickly and type new code in response to new demands or changes on stage.

\subsection{The grammar of performative coding}

%Now, I would like to mention some key points in consolidating the philosophy of live coding. How is it that there is a practice around the action of programming on stage?  

Live coding embraces a philosophy in which processes are the protagonists of the
performative act. Here, being able to see the process unfold, with a set of instructions
executed simultaneously, is also a way to see the artist's ideas and singularities.
There is a communication process between the interfaces, the performer, and the
public. The concept of liveness involves exploring the expressivity of coding while avoiding
a mechanical approach. Due to its openness, this concept has become political,
allowing one to modify the software and inviting new collectives to form. 

This practice operates at the intersection of the technical and artistic domains. Software
development has been an essential element in the evolution of this practice, and it
has enabled all the artistic experimentation and theorization. In live coding, the concept of users disappears. The practitioners are involved in the
creation, maintenance, and conceptualization of the software. The division between
the final product and the user cannot apply. This is the key point for diversity of
approaches within the practice. It allows for different kinds of disciplinary intersec-
tions.

Two key artists who helped establish live coding as a practice in the software and media art scene were software artist and musician Adrian Ward and programmer and artist Alex McLean. They became collaborators in the early 2000s while studying at the University of Plymouth in the UK. They shared the same interest and desire for programming to be a field of creativity and exploration. At that time, in the early 2000s, Ward won the Software Art Prize at Transmediale in 2001 for his project AutoIllustrator (2001), a semi-autonomous parody of Adobe Illustrator that generates visuals and challenges notions of programmable creativity. The following year, McLean won the same prize for Forkbomb.pl (2002), a simple script that visualizes crashing the machine on which it is run.

Ward and McLean explored the relationship between packaged and improvised software, which is why they were interested in creating their own software instead of using commercial, ready-to-use software. These explorations led to the formation of Slub, an experimental live coding group founded in 2000. Slub made their music exclusively from their own generative software and projected their screens so their audience could see their handmade interfaces. In September 2000, they presented The Generative Manifesto at a Perl community event held at the Institute of Contemporary Arts (ICA) in London. During the presentation, Ward recited the manifesto while McLean played a soundtrack of synthesized rhythms.
\cite{history} This manifesto establishes the principles and guidelines of their generative software practice and, at the same time, outlines key points in the practice of live coding, such as real-time output, code as an expressive medium, and the importance of transparency in processes. 

The Generative Manifesto was the seed of later explorations. Over the years, more artists and developers embraced the idea of the laptop as an expressive interface and worked to build environments that allow real-time output. For example, five years later, McLean developed Tidal Cycles, a domain-specific language focused on generating and manipulating audiovisual patterns, incorporating some of the ideas from this early manifesto. The points from the Generative Manifesto were as follows:
\cite{generativeManifesto}

\begin{itemize}
   \renewcommand{\labelitemi}{--}
  \item  Attention to detail—that only hand-made generative music can allow (code allows you to go deeper into creative structures);
 \item Realtime output and compositional control—we hate to wait (it is inconceivable to expect non-realtime systems to exhibit signs of life);
  \item  Construct and explore new sonic environments with echoes from our own. (art reflects human narrative, code reflects human activity);
\item Open process, open minds—we have nothing to hide (code is unambiguous, it can never hide behind obscurity. We seek to abolish obscurity in the arts);
\item  Only use software applications written by ourselves—software dictates output, we dictate software (authorship cannot be granted to those who have not authored!)
\end{itemize}

From this Generative Manifesto, it is important to note how certain clear principles are established regarding the software used. Real time is directly linked to the liveness of software and compositional processes. The design of creative tools is emphasized, embracing the do-it-yourself philosophy. The tools used must respond to the specific needs of the artist, not the other way around, where the artist must adapt to and constrain an external design. There is also an emphasis on openness, which involves being clear and transparent about processes, showing the public what the artist is building on stage.

In February 2004, the Changing Grammars symposium was convened by artist Renate Wieser and musician Julian Rohrhuber at the HfbK (Hochschule für Bildende Künste Hamburg). The symposium provided an opportunity for live coders and artists to discuss their practice and form a community around it.  The festival was politically engaged, concerned with code as art and live audio programming, and focused on open-source as a form of collective thinking. The festival's description reads, "Some computer languages allow changing a running process on the fly by rewriting the code that defines it. Applied to computer music, this means one can write sound compositions while they are playing." \cite{grammars}
 During this symposium, various collaborative live coding practices, as well as workshops and talks, were carried out, focusing on the writing of code as a possibility for musical composition. Among the live coders who participated in this festival were Adrian Ward, Alex McLean, Alberto de Campo, and Nick Collins.

The Changing Grammar Symposium also provided an opportunity to network and organize around live coding. Toplap was formed during the symposium. The name is a play on words, switching the order of "laptop." The collective's full name is "The Temporary Organization for the Promotion of Live Algorithm Programming." At that time, the collective's main objectives were to create a public archive and connect artists and people interested in live coding. The collective's first actions were to create a wiki and a mailing list. They also started drafting the collective's manifesto, which is still a draft open to modifications. The manifesto served as a guide for creating methodologies within the practice and for establishing important principles to discuss at that time. These are the original points of the Toplap Manifesto.
\cite{history}

We demand:
\begin{itemize}
   \renewcommand{\labelitemi}{--}
  \item Give us access to the performer's mind, to the whole human instrument.
  \item Obscurantism is dangerous. Show us your screens
  \item Programs are instruments that can change themselves
  \item Code should be seen as well as heard, underlying algorithms viewed as well as their visual outcome.
  \item  Live coding is not about tools. Algorithms are thoughts. Chainsaws are tools. That’s why algorithms are sometimes harder to notice than chainsaws.
\end{itemize} 
We recognise continuums of interaction and profundity, but prefer: 
\begin{itemize}
   \renewcommand{\labelitemi}{--}
  \item Insight into algorithms
    \item  The skillful extemporisation of algorithm as an expressive/impressive display of mental dexterity
    \item No backup (minidisc, DVD, safety net computer)
\end{itemize} 
 We acknowledge that: 
\begin{itemize}
   \renewcommand{\labelitemi}{--}
  \item    It is not necessary for a lay audience to understand the code to appreciate it, much as it is not necessary to know how to play guitar in order to appreciate watching a guitar performance.
   \item  Live coding may be accompanied by an impressive display of manual dexterity and the glorification of the typing interface.
    \item Performance involves continuums of interaction, covering perhaps the scope of controls with respect to the parameter space of the artwork, or gestural content, particularly directness of expressive detail. Whilst the traditional haptic rate timing deviations of expressivity in instrumental music are not approximated in code, why repeat the past? No doubt the writing of code and expression of thought will develop its own nuances and customs.
\end{itemize} 


In the Toplap Manifesto, we see that the process plays a privileged role in live coding, and that the call for transparency is also political. From there, the idea of starting from scratch became more prevalent as a form of expression. There is also a demand for the performative experience to be completely ephemeral and for no backups of the code to be made.

Currently, Toplap is an umbrella organization that brings together numerous artists and collectives. It connects projects from around the world. Although there are numerous nodes in different countries that are connected to each other, they have developed their own approaches to live coding. The community has grown and undertaken major initiatives, such as the International Conference on Live Coding (ICLC), which began in 2015 at the University of Leeds in the United Kingdom. Since then, the conference has been held annually or biennially, depending on the needs of the organization in each location. The conference is organized by various entities, including universities and collectives interested in researching live coding. During the conference, numerous concerts, workshops, and talks are held, and it also serves as a networking and knowledge-sharing space. Each conference edition has different thematic focuses. For example, the ICLC 2025 theme was "liveness" along with some session topics such as: "Specialized Tools and DSLs," "Live Coding Beyond Computers and Interfaces," "Audio-Visual Live Coding," "Speculative Futures," "Communities and Artistic Practices," and "Collaborative Live Coding." These topics reflect on liveness and its many forms and consequences within live coding, addressing the tension between what is live and what is not.  Taking into consideration the different dimensions in which live coding develops, such as its performative character, the construction of collective and community, and its different hybridizations and intersections with other practices

The Generative Manifesto and Toplap have shaped and established certain structures that have significantly favored the development of live coding. For example, the idea of using self-created software has generated numerous live coding environments with different functionalities and approaches. When writing their own software, artists can more efficiently address their own interests and needs. The idea of transparency and openness has led to certain aesthetic and methodological choices, such as projecting the code and making it visible to the public, as well as starting from scratch. Prioritizing expressive algorithmic processes over final products leads to embracing error. The concept of a score shifts, and the compositional process takes place during the performance, so musical or visual pieces do not correspond to a representation and are the result of improvisation based on the ideas the performer develops on stage. The liveliness also resides in the software's ability to provide real-time output of the actions performed by the artist, and in the artist interacting dynamically with the software during the performance, giving protagonism to the conversation generated between the interface and the artist.


\subsection{Programming livenness}


Live coding employs Domain-Specific Languages (DSLs), defined as computer languages that are designed for specific applications. In the context of live coding, the objective of these languages is to enhance the performative aspect by facilitating more intuitive and expressive coding. In the domain of sound live coding, for instance, these languages possess the capacity to modify and combine incoming audio streams and/or stored samples through the written execution of lines of code that are readily comprehensible to individuals with a background in sound and music. 
An illustrative example of this would be the naming of effects after sound-related terminology. This intuitive nature is particularly important for artists from non-programming or coding backgrounds.
\cite{puckette2007theory}
\footnote{For example, in the case of a distortion effect within a livecoding environment, the term 'distort' would most likely be used while coding.}

In the summer of 2025, Tina Frank started a class on analog synthesizers at Kunstuniversität. I took the class to experiment with the university's new acquisition, the video synthesizer, which arrived that semester. The synthesizer grew over the semester with new modules to experiment with. 
This visual synthesizer has oscillators of different shapes that produce colors and geometries at certain frequencies. You can adjust these frequencies using MIDI or Control Voltage (CV). Some examples of CVs are waveshapers, ramps, and amplifiers.\cite{dreher2014history} 

A number of video synthesiser prototypes inspired by music synthesizers were developed in the late 1960s and 1970s. 
For instance, in 1968, Eric Siegel, a pioneer in the field, started working on the Processing Chrominance Synthesizer (PCS). 
This was the first colouriser to separate black-and-white signals from cameras, videocassettes or broadcast signals, and add colour 
to them.\cite{gibson2022live} Another notable example is the Rutt/Etra Scan Processor, which was designed in 1973 by Steve Rutt,
 Bill Etra and Louise Etra. This is an analogue computer designed to manipulate the deviation signal that generates the raster scanning
  in the cathode ray tube of the monitor in real time.\cite{etra1992rutt} Steina and Woody Vasulka pioneers in experimental video used 
  Rutt/Etra Scan Processor to expand the scope of their experiments with electronic imaging. In an interview, Woody Vasulka talks about 
  the reasons he started experimenting with video synthesis and the temporal aspects that distinguish this type of experimentation from 
  film and cinema. "Video has an immediacy and an aspect of time manipulation. The frame can be altered, as well as the color, keying, 
  and feedback. All of these aspects elude the modalities of the filmic image." \cite{honeycuttdeep} One of the central themes of the Vasulkas' work was the temporality and liveliness of the systems they used. They created performances and installations in which human interaction brought technological systems and human manipulation into dialogue. When it came to activating them, both parties had a degree of creative agency.

When using an analog synthesizer, you have to plug in a source, which can be a shape, oscillation, or an external video source. Then, you can try effects like waveshapers and RGB signals. There's another way to alter the video signals: create feedback using your output as a source. You can also achieve this type of effects and feedback with Hydra. During Tina Frank’s class, I tried different modulations, ramps and feedback patches in the analog synthesizer, which was a nice experience that helped me understand the operations I've been performing with Hydra in a more tactile way.\footnote{You can see a video of a patch I made in the Visual Synth class here: \includegraphics[height=20mm]{synthcode.png}}

I would like to explain how this feedback would in Hydra notation. Take the following piece of code, for example:
\[osc(10).thresh().invert(() => Math.sin(time)).out(o0)\]  
osc() stands for oscillation that is the source. In the middle are thresh() and invert(), which affect the source. The last thing is the output, out().\footnote{You can try out this code in the hydra web \href{https://hydra.ojack.xyz}https://hydra.ojack.xyz}

Now, I will take a modulation. Here, you must select a source again. It can be a shape or an oscillator. Here, however, we will use our output o0 to create feedback and we placed it as modulate(o0).
\[osc(10).thresh().modulate(o0).invert(() => Math.sin(time)).out(o0)\]

If you try the code on the Hydra website, you'll see that this small change of routing the output inside a modulation creates a loop that generates patterns like trails. Other results that can be achieved using feedback include fractals or infinite zooms.

Another live coding environment that I would like to mention is Tidal Cycles. 
This was developed by Alex McLean during his PhD at Goldsmiths, University of London, 
between 2007 and 2011. 
The idea behind this environment is based on patterns within sound. 
It uses SuperDirt as a framework to function as a sampler, and SuperCollider to provide 
backup functionality and audio processing.\cite{tidal2022}

When you create a rhythm in Tidal Cycles, it repeats. You can divide the patterns into smaller sections of time, 
but the structure is cyclical, not linear. One cycle follows another. This idea is especially good in some types of electronic music, 
which are based on constant repetition. The program's library includes various drum machines, synthesizers, 
and bass sounds that are commonly used in this genre. This made people start to mix electronic music with other types of music. 
This is how Algorave events were created. First coined by Alex McLean and Nick Collins in 2011, 
the term algorave seeks to bring together the cultures of rave parties and algorithmic music. 
It provides a platform for performances and parties based exclusively on live coding. Algorave events are live electronic dance music performances. 
\cite{tidal}

\section{Show your Screen}
\vspace{.5cm}

I will talk about some of my own performances and those I have taken part in. I will identify the different working methods and discoveries that appeared during the process. I would also like to explore the different qualities of time in various performance modes and address the live aspect of live coding, considering its potential for experimentation. I would also like to talk about the importance of community in live coding and how this can lead to new approaches to time and performance.

In general, one of the fundamental principles of live coding performances is live improvisation. Algorithms are created and manipulated in real time to generate what the audience sees and hears, either from scratch or by modifying pre-existing code in response to the specific context of the performance. Another key element is demonstrating to the audience that what they are witnessing is being created live. This is why the performer's screen is usually projected, revealing everything, including mistakes.

Even though one of the main definitions of live coding in its early days was improvisation from scratch, not all live coders start from a 'blank page', demonstrating exceptional skill and speed. Some use prepared code. 
So do I, as it makes it easier to control the sounds and images, and prevents the programmes from becoming overloaded, crashing or producing poor sound quality. In addition I don't entirely agree with purist live coders who disapprove of prepared code in live performances. However, I somehow understand and would argue in a similar way, that 'from scratch' performances have a special quality in terms of time, disposition towards the live
coding tool, and interaction with the audience and other artists, particularly
when there is more than one performer on stage.

Before a performance, I spend time rehearsing and planning what I want to do, the sounds I am going to use and the images or textures that will be on stage. This process is similar to painting: you prepare a canvas, select a colour palette and choose a motif. However, other experiences within the practice escape this type of prior ideation and are subject to the moment itself and the unpredictability of the people and elements involved in the performance. Examples include jam sessions, collective performances and 'from scratch' performances.

\subsection{Dusk of an electric forest}
\vspace{.5cm}

'Dusk of an Electric Forest' was a performance piece that I created for the 'Sound Fever' event at the Ars Electronica Campus in 2024.  The call for submissions for that edition invited reflection on questions such as:

\begin{itemize}
  \renewcommand{\labelitemi}{--}
  \item What is the sound of the Anthropocene? How do we listen to the Anthropocene?
  \item How do we listen to ourselves in the Anthropocene?
  \item How does the past resonate in the future? What are the future sounds of our digital and physical selves?
  \item How can music alter narratives and adapt to the constantly shifting ecosystem in which it is created? Is it possible to change the listener's attention into a shared space and an imaginative space? 
  \item Can we decentralize the human realm, amplifying the voices of non-human agents?
\end{itemize}

When conceptualising the performance, I used the sounds of a hyper-technological society as my starting point: noise, the incessant hum of construction, machines that shake the ground, the saturation of cities and silence — the decline or absence of other species.

The Anthropocene has impacted numerous ecosystems, and the concept of human expansion and growth has led to a constant state of emergency. We are suffering the consequences of our own actions, and paradoxically, our surroundings become quieter amid our own noise in these environments that we have modified.
\footnote{You can see the video documentation of the performance here:
  \includegraphics[height=20mm]{frame.png}}

I would like to mention the work of Bernie Krause on biophony and soundscapes. 
In the late 1960s, he began researching and documenting the sounds of natural ecosystems around the world. 
His archive includes ecosystems that have since been wiped out by human activity. 
He has collected over 5,000 hours of recordings from natural environments, 
including at least 15,000 terrestrial and marine species from around the world \cite{ribac2016bernie}.

He took his orchestra idea to a fellow scientist, the late Stuart Gage. 
Together, they developed the acoustic niche hypothesis, which posits that living beings that share an ecosystem
 evolve to produce sounds with different rhythms and tones so as not to interfere with each other \cite{krause2016wild}. 
 This is important for finding mates, food and water.
  It is a harmonious system in which different species coexist alongside their diverse sounds, which are astonishingly beautiful. 

Listening to Bernie's early recordings from the 1970s instantly evokes a sense of nostalgia for a distant,
  highly diverse world that is far removed from city life. Although many ecosystems remain intact, many are under threat.  The sound of cars or air traffic makes it difficult for many species to hear each other, leaving them vulnerable to predators or making it difficult for them to mate. And as for us, our noise pollution is often unbearable. While cities may have an interesting soundscape, rapid growth or change leads to noise becoming predominant.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{rain.jpg}
  \caption{Dzanga Rainforest, Central African Republic. Bernie Krause. Spectrogram showing different species of animals.}
\end{figure}

Krause made his first sound recording of Sugarloaf State Park in Sonoma Valley, California, 
with the intention of documenting the environment near his home. From then on, he regularly 
recorded that park. In 2011, the area suffered a severe drought that lasted for years.
 As can be seen in Figure 2, the spectrogram shows how the soundscape gradually falls silent: 
 birds no longer sing as they once did because they have to conserve energy due to food shortages,
  while the insect population declines and the vegetation dries up. Finally, in 2017, 
  the forest fire broke out, affecting much of the area.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{specto.png}
  \caption{Spectrogram of the soundscape of Sugarloaf State Park, California, from 2011 to 2017. Bernie Krause.}

Silence is also a homogeneous mass of noise. In Dusk of an Electric Forest, I explored the idea that silence is not an absence of sound or a pause, but an overlapping mass of noise. To achieve this, I recorded sounds from construction sites in the city, such as the noise of drills, the movement of heavy machinery across asphalt, and car alarms. I also recorded the Traun River flowing and insects singing, for example.

During the performance, moments of unintelligible, 
grainy murmurs can be heard, which then become more rhythmic and repetitive. The performance begins with the sounds of insects moving from one source to another in stereo. Samples of drills and hammers are mixed with the continuous murmur of water.

The visuals feature a glitch grid that resembles landscapes or neon cartographies. 
As the performance progresses, these small squares change position and colour, and the textures rotate and become increasingly grainy, 
in line with the sound.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{unnamed.png}
  \caption{Hydra visual from Dusk of an Electric Forest.}

Drawing inspiration from the mythical figure of the snake woman or the goddess Coatlicue, 
as described by Gloria Anzaldúa in Borderlands: The Frontier, the new mestiza is a being that bridges destruction and fertility \cite{anzaldua2021borderlands}, and is resistance itself. A writhing snake moves through these hyper-glitched landscapes, falling and approaching the audience with its mouth open and fangs exposed.

In pre-Hispanic cosmogony, the snake symbolises fertility and acts as a guide between life and death. For instance, there is the legend of Bachué, the woman who founded the villages of the Bogotá savannah. Once her children were ready for her departure, she plunged into the river, transformed into a snake, and disappeared forever.

Just as snakes shed their skin, we are constantly changing and leaving behind the ruins of our past selves. However, it is interesting to consider where we are going each time we shed our old skin. While we are capable of chaos, we are also capable of achieving balance. This female figure also symbolises rebellion and care.

  \caption{Picture from Dusk of an Electric Forest performance. Photo by Flavia Somarriba}

For me, Anzaldua has been a key figure in feminism and philosophy. In her writing, she reimagines Mexican and Chicano spirituality from the perspective of historical and political violence and domination. She is also a key figure when it comes to issues of identity related to mestizaje and borders. Gloria Anzaldua explores forms of resistance by critically and constantly reconfiguring our identity formations and ontological categories, with the aim of achieving positive social change and global justice.

For this performance, I aimed to transform the chaotic sounds of the Anthropocene into repetitive, rhythmic noise by turning them into grainy, elongated and distorted sounds. I selected sounds that typically accompany urban growth, such as construction work, alarms, and machinery. These sounds are very prevalent today, as megaprojects often take precedence over architectural and landscape conservation. Despite all this noise, other small animals, such as insects and birds, can still be heard, finding a place in our fast-paced way of life.

But how can we think about the silence of the Anthropocene when it is spreading and dominating other species and natural cycles? Perhaps one day our species will no longer have this desire to spread without limits, and our sounds will once again become diverse.

Another key aspect of this performance was considering those symbols that resist Western homogenisation and colonial processes. We should ask ourselves what we can learn from cosmogonies that seem so distant to us, how identity can be built from miscegenation, mixtures, and exchanges, and how we can update our own worldviews.

Although we are aware of the environmental emergency we are living in, it seems that our relationship with accelerated change remains unchanged. Perhaps in the future, we will either become a homogeneous mass of noise or make room for other elements to emerge more organically.

On stage, I sought order once again, rediscovered the sounds I had planned, modified the code I had already written, ran it, commented on the sections I wanted to mute or might need later, changed parameters, and also gave myself the freedom to improvise with the palette I had selected.

Finally, to mention some technical information, in this performance I used two live coding environments: Hydra for the visuals and Tidal Cycles for the sound. \subsection{Empty Beginning}

The 'from scratch' approach is playful and embraces error. 
There is no pre-established order, and a musical composition is created in real time. Starting with a 'blank sheet', the performance itself then develops.

At the same time, the audience participates and witnesses the performer developing their ideas, 
with algorithms serving as the primary notation. Audience members do not need to be familiar with programming to enjoy the performance. 
The visible code reveals artistic thought 'in action', embedded in the materiality and mediality of the performance itself \cite{melete}.

For me, the first few minutes of a performance 'from scratch' can be overwhelming, with that initial silence and an audience that is both expectant and sometimes confused. Then movement appears on the screen: the performer begins to write, and from that moment on, the performance starts in earnest. The live coder continues to write, and as the text transforms, so do the sounds and images.

During my live coding practice, the presence of a collective and a community that organises events, innovates in the field, experiments and teaches has been crucial. Toplap is a collective founded in Hamburg in 2004 that has been dedicated to promoting and practising live coding ever since. Toplap has expanded worldwide in the form of local nodes that organise events, workshops, concerts and meetings. Anyone can start a new Toplap node.

This global live coding community organises events that seek to connect artists from different parts of the world. Numerous online events are broadcast live, including Livecodera, Toplap Solstice and SuperCollider meetups. The most important event is the International Conference of Live Coding, which is organised every year in a different location, bringing together performers and academics to share their research and artistic projects.

When Gorka Egino and I participated in the Toplap Solstice in December 2024, we were in different locations. For this performance, we decided not to have any prior ideas or plans for our session. I was in charge of the sound, while Gorka was in charge of the visuals. We used Flok, a collaborative, web-based live coding editor that allows several people to connect to a session via the same link.

\subsection{Toplap Linz}

In June 2024, I had the opportunity to teach a workshop on Tidal Cycles with the NoiseMeetup collective. 
This was my first time teaching a workshop on this tool, and I invited Gorka Egino to collaborate with me. 
We used Flok.cc to connect all the participants to the same platform. First, we provided an introduction, covering how the tool works, how to create initial sounds and how to generate compositions using code. The second part consisted of playing together using Flok.

From that first experience, we learned that in order to start a jam, certain rules must
 be established, as having more than 15 people play together on the same interface 
 can quickly lead to chaos. This workshop also led to the creation of Toplap Linz.
  Founded in 2004 in the UK, TOPLAP  (Transnational Organisation for the Parsimony of Live Art Programming)
  is an organisation that explores and promotes live coding.
   This collective functions like a rhizome: anyone with the necessary motivation 
   and knowledge can start a Toplap group and establish a community spread all over the world.

TOPLAP Linz is a TOPLAP node located in Linz, Austria. It was founded on 2024 as part of the broader international TOPLAP community to explore and promote live coding practices. It is aligned with the open-source philosophy and one of its core pillars are being inclusive and accessible.

As a result of the creation of Toplap Linz, we also started organising meetings to answer questions, share knowledge and try new things. However, these meetings were not as well received, so after a few attempts, we began to wonder if our reach was limited, or if the invitations were ambiguous or unappealing.

Some time later, in collaboration with Noise Meetup, we held a second two-day workshop. We repeated the workshop and jam format, and this time, as well as teaching Tidal Cycles, we included Hydra. This event was held at DH5. We dedicated the first day to workshops and the second to a computer jam. We had more participants this time, and overall it was a successful event — we were all writing code and having fun by the end!

This time, we made sure to install Tidal Cycles on the computers of those attending for the first time, despite knowing it would be time-consuming and potentially cumbersome. We felt it was important for people to have the tool ready to use outside the workshop, in other contexts. We also wanted to provide a space for group installation, as this process can be confusing if you don't have experience with operating system command applications. Our goal was to make the experience as enjoyable as possible from the outset.

Then, we got down to business and shared a Flok.cc session where everyone could connect at the same time and review the code.
 We also created a cheat sheet for Tidal Cycles and Hydra with guidelines to help participants start exploring the tool. 
 It's a simple guide offering an overview of the possibilities of these two environments 
 for experimenting with sound and visuals respectively (see fig. 9).
From my experience of learning to use different software,
 I have personally found it quite useful to be able to follow the process on my own computer. 
 This allows you to interact more effectively with the tools when someone explains something in a masterclass.

On the second day of the workshop, we devoted ourselves solely to computer jam sessions. We timed each session, starting with five minutes, then moving on to ten, twenty and thirty minutes. In the longer sessions, we worked with themes. For instance, in one session, we used the symbol '520', which is used in China to say 'I love you'. The visuals and sound were often humorous and corny. In another session, someone suggested using horses. Interesting things happened in this dynamic and we were all able to collaborate. Sometimes, after a jam, questions arose about how to create a particular rhythm or apply an effect to the visuals. We realised that, in the jam dynamic, people dared to try more complex things.
 
As a general rule, we decided not to copy code from sources outside our Flok page, such as the official website or online tutorials. There were two main reasons for this. The first is that it is difficult to predict the result of mixing pieces of code that are not understood — it may not work at all. The second is that if you join someone else's code to yours, it will most likely break and stop working. Therefore, we focused on the process of crafting the code, adopting a slower but more deliberate approach to ideation and elaboration. We always started from scratch and developed the jam together.

Another important principle that applies to any type of jam session is the importance of active listening. This involves understanding each other while doing something together and knowing when to try a sound from the library and how to introduce it. When you are unsure of the sound you want to introduce, it is often better not to go in at full volume. You could also choose to play divisions and rhythms similar to those of others, or simpler divisions, before moving on to polyrhythms.

In a group of more than six people improvising simultaneously, 
it is important to understand that there are times when it is better to give others space and remain silent. 
These pauses are useful for taking a step back and observing what is happening without the pressure of having to keep programming. They also give you a moment to think about how you can contribute something new to the jam, such as another sound, rhythm, modification of the existing code, image or effect.

\subsection{Art as a series of steps}

In this chapter, I have taken a journey through different experiences within the practice of live coding, 
trying to cover different methodologies of conceptualization and realization. 
There have been different degrees of improvisation, planning, and execution. 
This has also been a journey of my own to understand what brings a practice such as programming 
to “life” and what happens when it collides with the living arts.

For me, the performative practice of live coding is a field of experimentation
 in which I invite the audience to observe how I construct what they are hearing and seeing, 
 in which the audience's gaze is active, they are witnesses to constant decision-making, 
 they can see when there is doubt, errors, skill, audacity, etc. The computer contains the body. 
 The body extends the computer. In this exercise of response and interaction, the body is not passive towards the interface; it is the body that activates it dynamically.

In my own practice, there has been ambivalence in terms of score, choreography, and improvisation. In some cases, such as my performance Dusk of an Electric Forest, there is a concept and a prior curation of images and sounds, the code is constructed from an established frame, and there is more or less an idea of different moments in the performance. 

However, even in this type of experience, the performance is not limited to the mechanical act of activating and deactivating lines of code. It involves working with a limited color palette, but with several parameters that remain open and escape any planning. Although in Dusk of an Electric Forest, I had tried different effects, rhythms, and patterns. When the moment of the performance arrived, things were different. Things that I thought would work suddenly didn't sound as I expected. Adrift, I had to find new ways to construct the sensations I wanted to convey and discard some pre-established ideas. A space of uncertainty opens up, and being flexible is the key to enjoying and constructing the performance.

Conditions change radically when you try or practice something alone, without an audience, and when you face the stage. Things look and sound different on speakers than on headphones, or on a computer screen and a projector. The acoustic and visual conditions are totally different, which means you have to be able to adapt.

\subsection{Vanishing photographs }

In my participation of Sankt interface 2024, I did a camera in which the image is never stable and with even little movements will start to blur and vanish, in every click you freeze a different moment but it will tend to disappear.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{Camila_Scholz_Sankt2024 (17).JPG}
  \caption{Picture of Vanishing photographs, Sankt Interface Festival 2024, Linz. Photo by Camila Scholz}
\end{figure}

I thought for this work on the difficulties of archiving and how memory is alsomething that we can not rely entirely. In a era where images are hyper abundant, we value thien less, somethings the photos that we take re forgotten in  a cloud or even worse just existing in pur celphone with the threaten of disappear if something happen to that device.

I would like to reference Susan Sontag's famous take on photography and its relationship with mortality and ephemerality. “All photographs are memento mori. To take a photograph is to participate in 
another person’s (or thing’s) mortality, vulnerability, mutability. Precisely by slicing out this moment and freezing it, all photographs testify to time’s relentless melt.”\cite{sontag2021photography}

For this project, I worked with a toy camera that I found on the street. It's a nostalgic object that was transformed into a mouse and regained its symbolic function of capturing images. This artifact is capable of freezing time and then also allows you to see how time resists being captured and the image finally vanish.

The computer is another element that acts as a mirror; one can only capture an image through their reflection. This narrows the possibilities of the artifact; the audience can only take self-portraits, which makes it more intimate. You don't just see how an image crumbles; you actually see yourself melting away.

On the computer, a JavaScript code is running within the Hydra web. There are two mouse interactions: the first freezes the image when clicked, and the second modulates the image when the mouse moves from side to side, producing the vanishing effect.
